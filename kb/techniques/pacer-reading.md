# PACER (reading & remembering framework)

**What it's for:** Remembering and using what you read. Reading has two stages —
**consumption** (information in) and **digestion** (encoding it into memory) — and
learning only occurs when they stay balanced. Most people optimize consumption (speed
reading, 3× lectures, binge audiobooks) and forget up to 90% of it: the learning
equivalent of overeating and vomiting it back up. What goes into your brain matters far
less than what stays. Remembering *everything* is impossible and the wrong goal anyway
(even Kim Peak's perfect recall came with weak reasoning); the goal is remembering what
you need, in a form you can use.

**When:** Any reading or listening session — textbook, paper, documentation, lecture.

**How:** While consuming, classify each piece of information into one of five
categories, then digest it with that category's targeted process:

- **P — Procedural** (how to do something: technique, code, language usage) →
  **Practice.** Apply it in real life as early as possible. Don't memorize instructions
  for later; if you can't practice now, stop consuming or move on — reading more how-to
  without doing it is waste.
- **A — Analogous** (reminds you of something you already know, including recurring
  patterns within the field) → **Critique.** Form the analogy, then interrogate it: how
  exactly are they similar? Different? Where does it break down? Extend or replace it if
  it's weak. Critiquing is the step everyone skips and where most of the value is.
- **C — Conceptual** (the "what": facts, theories, principles, relationships) →
  **Map.** Build nonlinear, networked notes (mind map). Conceptual knowledge inherently
  lives in a network — an expert can enter anywhere and navigate anywhere; the linear
  order of the textbook is an artifact. Your job is to recreate the expert's *network*,
  not their sequence. Grow and reorganize the map as you read.
- **E — Evidence** (details that make concepts concrete: statistics, cases, dates,
  worked examples) → **Store & rehearse.** Note it immediately (into the map, a second
  brain like Obsidian/Notion, or flashcards) and move on. Later, rehearse by *using* it:
  solve problems, write answers, teach, use it as a supporting example. Do NOT stall
  mid-reading to memorize it.
- **R — Reference** (nitty-gritty specifics that don't change understanding: constants,
  gene names, parameter lists) → **Store & rehearse** with direct fact recall —
  flashcards/Anki are the right tool here. Never burn reading time memorizing these.

**The balance rule:** Everything consumed must be digested. If there's no time to
digest with the right process, consume less — slowing intake is more efficient than
forgetting and reconsuming. E and R digest fast; P, A, and C are the time-consuming
parts and form the foundation the details hang off.

**Common mistakes:** Treating all information the same (rereading reference facts,
carding conceptual networks); consuming a whole lecture + textbook chapter before
digesting anything; skipping the critique step of analogies; letting E/R details eat
the reading session.

**Signs it's working:** You end reading sessions with a grown map, a small store of
details, and things you've already practiced — not a pile of unprocessed pages; days
later the structure is still there.
