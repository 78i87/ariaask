# Attention management (over time management)

"Lack of time" is almost never the real problem — humans barely sense time at all;
what we actually notice is a **lack of result**. Infinite time wouldn't fix it (we'd
schedule infinitely more). The failure is in how attention got directed, so the thing
to manage is attention, with time management as a thin layer on top — not the other way
around.

**The river metaphor:** time is a current you're floating down with a poor sense of its
speed. Time management plants checkpoints ("turn here at 7:30, finish the turn by 8") —
but if a turn runs long, or you never learned to turn well, the whole downstream plan
breaks. What actually matters is the ability to **steer**: react, adapt, and direct
attention effectively wherever in the river you are. That's why schedules alone
collapse for most people, while attention-managed people stay productive even when the
day goes sideways.

**Practice:**
- Ask, at transitions: *where is my attention right now, and where do I want it?*
  Deciding "nowhere, I'm resting" is a legitimate answer — the point is that it's a
  decision, not a drift.
- **Mobilize dead space**: commutes, queues, walks, elevators — time already locked
  into a physical activity but with attention free. Attention-only tasks fit there:
  planning the day, prioritizing, listening to learning material, mindfulness. People
  who "have no time to meditate" have a commute.
- **Mind the gaps between tasks**: finishing something and dissolving into the feed for
  an unmeasured while is where hours vanish. An intentional "attention handoff" at each
  boundary ("next, my attention goes to…") recovers them and — second benefit — trains
  the brain to associate transitions with re-entering flow, so focus itself improves.
- **Layer, in this order**: foundation = attention/focus management every day; sprinkle
  time/task techniques on top to "lubricate" it; bring out rigid time-blocking only for
  genuinely packed days and fixed appointments. Roughly 90% attention management, 10%
  clock.

**Coaching cues:**
- "I made a schedule and never follow it" → they built checkpoints with no steering;
  start with attention handoffs and dead-space use, reintroduce light scheduling later.
- "I don't have time to study/meditate/review" → inventory dead space first.
- A learner gluing themselves to Pomodoro timers while drifting mid-interval → the
  timer manages time, not attention; add the "where is my attention?" check.
