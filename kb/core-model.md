# The core learning model

This is the distilled framework behind every recommendation the coach makes. It is the
source of truth for the summary baked into the coach persona — keep the two in sync.

## 1. Studying is not learning

Studying is physical activity (reading, writing notes, testing); learning is the
cognitive outcome — and it has only happened if the material is **retained**,
**deeply understood**, and **applicable** the way the learner needs. Techniques sit on
a spectrum of learning-per-hour: rereading and highlighting produce almost none.
Always ask "how much learning is this method producing?", never "how many hours?"

## 2. The memory model

Information flows sensory memory → **working memory** → long-term memory. Working
memory is the workbench: it holds items for only ~15–30 seconds and has a severe
"weight limit" (a few items; heavy jargon costs more, familiar chunks cost less). What
you *do* in that window — the handling — decides whether information encodes into
long-term memory or is discarded. The brain aggressively forgets whatever it can't
place in a relevance structure; fighting that with repetition is rote memorization,
the tool of last resort. "Poor memory" is almost always poor memory handling.

## 3. Encoding beats review

Memory and understanding are **byproducts of organized networks** (schemas). Building
well-organized, interconnected structure makes remembering and understanding happen by
default; merely trying to memorize or understand does not. Strong encoding flattens the
forgetting curve at the source, cancelling most future revision — this is the hallmark
of "geniuses," and it is trainable (deep processing). The three pillars, in the order
to develop them: **enablers** (self-management + growth skills of experimentation and
reflection — the rate limiters), **retrieval** (the safety net), **encoding** (the
long game and the biggest prize).

## 4. Effort is the mechanism (cognitive load)

All effective learning involves high cognitive load; if studying feels easy, boring, or
drowsy, almost no learning is happening (the red flag). But load must come from
processing the material — comparing, organizing, generating — and stay within capacity:
overload (stuck, fog, rereading the same paragraph) produces nothing either. Learners
misread productive effort as "the method isn't working" (misinterpreted effort
hypothesis) and retreat to comfortable, useless methods. Desirable difficulty says the
zone just past comfort — meaningful mistakes, effortful recall — is where learning is
fastest.

## 5. The deep processing loop

The moment-to-moment habit of a strong encoder, run on every meaningful piece of
information, inside the working-memory window: **pause** (stop consuming; keep
consumption and digestion balanced — don't "overeat"), **simplify** (re-express
plainly), **compare** (against prior and new knowledge), **connect** (make links
explicit, on paper), **group** (collapse webs into named patterns), **judge** (decide
what matters most; treat every structure as a hypothesis to challenge). Failures of the
loop produce excellent questions. Notes exist to offload this loop ("think on paper"),
not to transcribe — nonlinear, low-word-count, structural.

## 6. Retrieval done right

Retrieval (any use of knowledge from memory) strengthens memory and finds gaps. Quality
order: **free recall > cued recall > recognition** (recognition is the ultimate
illusion of learning). Test **early and often** (weekly/monthly rhythm — exam results
should never be a surprise), **at the level you'll be used at** (Bloom's: analysis and
evaluation, not just facts), in **both declarative and procedural** forms, with **cues
matching real usage**. Recall quality matters more than spacing precision: strong free
recall decays slowly and tolerates loose spacing; weak cued recall demands constant
repetition.

## 7. Techniques are tools: match technique to task and stage

There is no best technique and no hack; there are tools with purposes and limitations,
assembled into a personal system (see guides/technique-selection.md for the full map):

- **Exact, isolated facts** → flashcards/SRS, memory palaces — powerful, narrow, last
  resort after encoding has minimized the rote residue; expanding gaps, and spacing
  flexibility is proportional to encoding quality.
- **Conceptual material** → priming, PACER reading, mind mapping (GRINDE), analogies.
- **Procedural material** → worked examples *early*, then faded practice, then full
  problems — the best early technique becomes counterproductive after the basics.
- **Deep understanding & gap-finding** → teach-back (this app's built-in Aria mode).
- **Transfer / curveball-proofing** → interleaving: the same topic from multiple
  cognitive angles (answer questions → write questions → write model answers → teach),
  never the same review method twice in a row.
- **Overwhelm** → thinner layers, the ladder method, clear-thinking triage.
- **Skills** → RAIL staging (relevance → awareness → iteration → lifelong), one or two
  new elements at a time, ~1:5 theory-to-practice, accuracy → consistency → speed.
- **Enablers (can't start / can't focus)** → distraction cheat sheet, trigger removal
  (cold turkey for procrastination addiction), daily focus-muscle training (FIT via
  mindfulness reps), attention management over time management. Enablers gate
  everything else — fix them before optimizing study techniques.

The whole-system frame is **PERRIO** — Priming, Encoding, Reference (park fine details
so they don't derail encoding), Retrieval, Interleaving, Overlearning (optional, last,
only for elite standards). Diagnose the weakest slot rather than tuning the strongest;
"what's the best technique?" is the wrong question — systems, not techniques.

## 8. Self-regulation

Beware the illusion of learning (pretty notes, card-making, recognition). Assume every
"silly mistake" is a real gap. Study ahead rather than catching up; prime before
consuming. Change one or two habits at a time and keep a learning log of experiments —
one strategy-level entry per study block (goal / strategy / result-gap / next move),
kept while a strategy works and changed only when the bottleneck changes. Slow is fast:
consumption is never the bottleneck — processing is.

## 8b. Scaffolds fade toward independence

Prompted thinking is fine — the benefit lives in *answering*, not in who asked — but
prompts must become internal or they breed prompt-dependent thinking (the
cue-dependency trap applied to metacognition). Progression: full prompt → prompt menu →
minimal prompt → unprompted habit. After answering any prompted question, name the
thinking move ("that was a comparison question") — that step converts guidance into
independence. Scaffold, never replace: an answer handed over before the learner has
thought is replaced processing and evaporates.

## 9. The AI rule (governs the coach itself)

AI **harms** learning when it does the organizing, comparing, or judging for the
learner — the resulting clarity isn't theirs and evaporates within weeks. AI **helps**
when it gathers/summarizes raw material for the learner to process, verifies a
hypothesis the learner already formed, or replaces a slow search. The coach therefore
prompts, scaffolds, and verifies — it does not hand over organization or answers the
learner hasn't worked for.
