# Worked examples → faded practice (procedural subjects)

**What it's for:** Learning procedural material (math, physics, coding — the "P" in
PACER) at the *right cost curve*. The core finding of cognitive-load research (the
worked-example effect): **novices learn a problem type faster by studying worked
solutions than by attempting problems cold**, because solving without a schema burns
working memory on blind search instead of on learning the solution structure. But the
effect **reverses with expertise** (the expertise-reversal effect): once the basic
schema exists, continuing to read worked examples adds little and *practicing problems*
is what builds fluency, retention, and transfer. This is the canonical case of
"technique–stage fit": the best technique early is among the worst later.

**When:**
- **Worked examples:** first exposure to a new problem type — you couldn't yet outline
  the solution steps yourself.
- **Faded practice / full problems:** as soon as examples start feeling obvious —
  effortless reading of solutions is a low-cognitive-load red flag (see cognitive-load
  doc); comfort means it's time to move up.

**How:**
1. **Study 2–3 worked examples actively, not passively.** Self-explain each step: why
   this step, why here, what would break without it? (Self-explanation is what
   separates learning from the illusion of reading.) Compare examples with each other:
   what's constant (the schema) vs. surface detail?
2. **Fade:** take examples with later steps removed and complete them; then examples
   with most steps removed; then full problems. Each rung shifts load from following
   to generating.
3. **Move fully to practice problems** with retrieval-quality effort: attempt before
   peeking, and treat every "silly mistake" as a real gap (see self-regulation).
   Cover the declarative side too — Sung's warning for math: pure procedural drilling
   collapses on conceptual questions, so also test "what/why/when" understanding, not
   just execution.
4. **Vary the practice** (mixed problem types, changed contexts) once accuracy is
   consistent — matching RAIL's iteration stage.

**Common mistakes:** Grinding practice problems from day one ("struggle is good, right?"
— struggle without a schema is undesirable difficulty, mostly noise); still rereading
worked solutions weeks in (comfortable, feels productive, teaches recognition not
generation); mistaking "I followed the solution" for "I could produce the solution."

**Signs the switch point has arrived:** You predict the next step of examples before
reading it; solutions feel obvious. Signs the technique is working post-switch: rising
accuracy on unseen problems; errors become specific and diagnosable.
