# Scaffolds to independence (prompted vs self-generated thinking)

The learning benefit of an encoding question ("how does this compare?", "what does this
affect?") comes overwhelmingly from **doing the mental work of answering it** — not from
who asked it. Being prompted is fine, *as long as the answering, comparing, judging, and
organizing stay yours*. The boundary is **scaffold vs replacement**: a prompt that makes
you think is a scaffold; an answer handed over before you've thought is a replacement,
and replaced processing produces the illusion of competence.

But self-generating the question adds a second skill: **metacognition** — recognizing
*what kind of thinking the situation needs* ("these two tables look similar → this needs
comparison"; "this idea is floating → this needs connection"). Prompted questions teach
the current material; self-generated questions also train you to learn future material.
Having no questions at all when facing complex new material is a red flag — it means
you're not yet thinking at the level where questions arise.

## The dependency risk (cue-dependent thinking)

Just as cued recall breeds cue-dependent forgetting, always-prompted thinking breeds
prompt-dependent thinking: deep processing that fires only when someone asks. The goal
is not "never use prompts" — it is **use prompts until the prompts become internal**.

## The training-wheels progression

1. **Full prompt** — the exact question is supplied ("How does this compare to what you
   know?"); you answer it. Right for beginners at encoding.
2. **Prompt menu** — you choose which move fits from a menu (simplify? compare?
   connect? judge? apply? prerequisite check?). Trains selection.
3. **Minimal prompt** — only "do encoding"; you diagnose what the material needs.
4. **No prompt** — you notice on your own: "this is floating, connect it"; "these look
   similar, compare"; "I'm confused, zoom out." The destination.

**The internalizing step:** after answering any prompted question, add one meta-question
— *"why was this the right question to ask here?"* Naming the thinking move ("that was a
comparison question", "a source-of-truth question") is what converts guided practice
into independent skill.

## Don't checklist it

Running "simplify ✓ compare ✓ connect ✓ judge ✓" on everything is shallow compliance.
The right question is "which of these moves would actually help me understand *this*
idea?" — sometimes the answer is compare, sometimes apply, sometimes "store it as
reference and move on."

## For this app specifically

This principle is the design law for learner modes and guided reading: highlights and
prompts are stage-1/2 scaffolds. The app should supply the question but never the
answer; nudge the learner to name the thinking move; and as usage accumulates, fade
from full prompts → menus → minimal prompts → post-session suggestions only — because
the pause–simplify–compare–connect–judge loop is supposed to end up in the learner's
head, not in the UI.

## Coaching cues

- Learner asks the coach to "just explain it" → scaffold, don't replace; and after they
  answer, ask them to name the move.
- Learner works well in-session but not alone → they're prompt-dependent; move them one
  stage down the progression.
- Learner reports the loop "firing on its own" → celebrate it and reduce prompting;
  that's the graduation signal.
