# Clear thinking under complexity

Thinking clearly is a trainable skill, not a talent. Justin Sung identifies three traps
that intelligent, high-functioning people fall into when a problem or decision feels
overwhelming, plus the counter-moves for each. These apply directly to learning: feeling
stuck on hard material is usually a process problem, not an ability problem.

## Trap 1: "When you're stuck, think harder"

Feeling stuck is usually a signal of cognitive overload, not insufficient effort. Working
memory is the workbench of the brain and it is severely limited for every human — roughly
a handful of items at once. Trying to hold five interacting factors simultaneously
overloads it; that overload is experienced as confusion and being stuck. Thinking harder
while overloaded makes clarity worse, because each fatiguing pass degrades the next.

**Counter-move: think less at once.** Limit thinking to two or three factors at a time,
resolve how those connect, then bring in the next piece. This mirrors "separation of
concerns" from computer science: resolving many unresolved pieces simultaneously is
confusing, while resolving each component alone and then recombining is simple. The
mistake is postponing this separation — as soon as you feel stuck, immediately narrow
what you are thinking about rather than pushing through.

## Trap 2: Treating overwhelm as a capacity problem

Overwhelm is not a capacity problem, it is a triage signal. The feeling means "I don't
know what to do next," not "I can't do all of this." Asking "how do I get through all of
it?" blocks the decisions that actually move the needle. You do not need to know how
everything connects to know what to do next.

**Counter-move: triage via the theory of constraints.** Ask: what is the one biggest
constraint that, if resolved or removed, would make everything else simpler and easier?
This focuses limited working memory on one thing.

**Technique: dependency mapping.** Write down all the factors, concepts, events, and
considerations making the situation difficult (a full brain dump), then draw arrows for
which factors cause or block others. The biggest constraints become visually obvious.
Useful for decisions, project planning, prioritization, and choosing what to learn next.

**Schedule time for non-urgent constraints.** Block a small amount of recurring time
(even one hour a week) for constraints that are long-term needle-movers but not urgent
yet. If a constraint becomes urgent in six months and takes six months to unblock, it is
urgent now. Learning to learn is the canonical example: hours spent weekly on reading
and studying means a 20–30% efficiency gain compounds for the rest of your life.

## Trap 3: Not working in confidence intervals

People try to reach a confident conclusion by being more precise, meticulous, and
detailed — and either become overconfident (ignoring inconvenient factors) or paralyzed
(endless analysis, never deciding). In complex, messy systems, 100% confidence in a
precise claim is not achievable.

**Counter-move: aim for accuracy, not confidence, and adjust the interval.** Confidence
is a byproduct of scope: a broader claim with a wider margin of error can be held with
near-total confidence. Shrink the claim until you are honestly confident in it, then plan
and act within that range instead of committing to a plan that only works if a precise
guess is right. Real high performers instinctively reframe to "what conclusion can I
confidently make right now with the information available, while staying honest with
myself?" Acknowledging uncertainty makes clear thinking easier, not weaker.

## Companion habit: think on paper

Offload working memory onto paper. Externalizing factors and connections (notes, maps,
diagrams) makes complex problems dramatically easier because the paper holds what working
memory cannot. Keep a notebook for exactly this.

## Coaching cues

- Learner says "I've been staring at this for an hour and it makes no sense" → cognitive
  overload; have them isolate two or three pieces and resolve those first (Trap 1).
- Learner says "there's too much to cover, I don't know where to start" → triage signal;
  find the biggest constraint, consider a dependency map (Trap 2).
- Learner won't move forward until their plan/understanding is perfect → widen the
  interval; act on what they can confidently claim now (Trap 3).
