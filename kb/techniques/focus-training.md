# Focus training (the focus muscle, distraction threshold, FIT)

**What it's for:** Focus on command — sitting down and being in concentrated work
within minutes, even tired, even unwilling. Focus is a **process, not a state**, and
the underlying capacity (the "focus muscle") is trainable; it does not improve on its
own, and the modern environment actively weakens it. Inconsistent focus doesn't just
waste sessions — it makes backlogs unpredictable, which destroys medium-term planning
and the feeling of control.

**The model:** Distractibility fluctuates around a **distraction threshold**; below it
you can do meaningful work, above it you can't. Perfect focus isn't the goal — a short
**re-entry time** is. Flow breaks constantly and normally; if re-entry takes 5 minutes,
a 3-hour block is ~85–90% productive, but at 30-minute re-entry the same block is ~50%
— and worse, the same forces that slow re-entry also shorten each flow window (often to
~15 minutes), so the block yields 30–40 minutes of real work. Two force sets act on
you: forces pushing distractibility up (remove these first) and forces pulling you into
focus (train these second — that order, or staying focused costs so much effort it
collapses by week two).

**Step 1 — the distraction cheat sheet (external, works immediately):**
Try a focus session with a sheet of paper beside you; every time you break out of flow,
write what pulled you out. Two categories: **environmental** (notifications, noise,
visual clutter, discomfort — heat, chair) and **interactive** (people walking in, calls,
requests — including the mere *knowledge* that someone might interrupt, or that a task
is pending). People over-optimize environmental and ignore interactive — but no
technique keeps you in flow through a person talking to you, so set explicit boundaries
and expectations with the people involved. Work down the list session by session.
Rhythmic audio (neural entrainment — steady rhythmic sound or music) both masks noise
and actively pulls toward focus.

**Step 2 — train the muscle (FIT):**
The brain fires stray thoughts every second or two; distraction is **fixation** — a
thought latches and drags you off. A strong focus muscle doesn't reduce the thoughts;
it makes the *snap-back* so fast you barely notice being pulled. Training = reps of
being pulled away and bringing yourself back, meeting the neuroplasticity conditions
**F-I-T**: Frequent reps (constant pull-away/return, not one per 20 minutes), Intense
enough stimulus (a stray thought already suffices for most), and enough Time (months —
like a gym habit).

The exercise is mindfulness meditation stripped to its mechanics: sit, eyes closed,
attend to the breath (or a repeated word — any anchor). You'll be distracted within
seconds; noticing and returning to the anchor **is one rep** — being distracted is the
point, not failure. 10–15+ minutes daily (20–30 for procrastination addicts); expect
no visible effect for ~a month, then compounding gains for years, spilling into
procrastination, emotional regulation, and sleep. Five distraction-free seconds is
genuinely advanced.

**Common mistakes:** Trying to "not get distracted" by willpower; training the muscle
while distraction forces are still maxed (do external first); quitting in week two
because meditation "isn't working" (the frustration is the rep); optimizing playlists
and apps while a housemate can walk in at any time.

**Signs it's working:** Re-entry time shrinks; you catch drifts earlier; blocked time
reliably produces output, so plans start meaning something.
