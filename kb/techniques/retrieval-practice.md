# Retrieval practice (test early, often, at the right level)

**What it's for:** Retention and gap-finding. Retrieval is any use of knowledge from
memory — answering, explaining, solving, writing. Each successful retrieval strengthens
the memory; each failed one exposes a gap while there's still time to fix it. Recall
failures are often retrieval failures rather than storage failures, and only practice
fixes retrieval.

**When:** Continuously — not the week before the exam. There is no good reason not to
know how well you'll do on an exam before walking in; if results surprise you, you're
testing too late, too rarely, or in the wrong way.

**How:**
1. **Test early and often.** Rhythm that works: test yourself at the end of every week
   on that week's material, and at the end of every month on the month's. Gaps found
   weekly are few and cheap to fix; gaps found the night before the exam are neither.
   Don't avoid hard questions you suspect you'll fail — the exam will ask them anyway.
2. **Prefer free recall over cued recall over recognition.** Three kinds of recall:
   *free* (open-ended — write everything you know, explain a topic, essay-style),
   *cued* (a question or fill-in-the-blank triggers the memory), *recognition* ("yeah,
   that looks right" — nearly worthless, and the ultimate illusion of learning when
   used to excuse a failed flashcard). The learning benefit concentrates in free recall.
3. **Test at the level you'll be tested at** (Bloom's revised taxonomy). Low-level =
   isolated facts, definitions, regurgitation. High-level = dissecting a complex
   question, choosing which concepts are relevant, and connecting them. Drilling only
   low-level questions while facing high-level exams guarantees struggling exactly on
   the complicated questions. Ask: *how will I need to use this knowledge?* — and test
   that way.
4. **Test both declarative and procedural knowledge.** Declarative = the "what"
   (concepts, definitions, when-to-use); procedural = the "how" (actually solving,
   actually writing code, actually performing). Math students often drill procedure and
   collapse on conceptual questions; theory-heavy learners know "about" the skill but
   can't execute. Cover whichever side your testing has neglected.
5. **Match cues to real usage.** If the knowledge must surface for complex questions,
   discussions, or decisions, don't train it exclusively on flashcard cues — cue
   mismatch blocks transfer (cue-dependent forgetting).

**Golden tip:** Recall *quality* matters more than spacing precision. Strong free-recall
practice slows knowledge decay so much that spacing can be loose (weeks); weak
cued/recognition review decays so fast it demands daily repetition. Fix the recall
method before optimizing the schedule.

**Common mistakes:** Checking the answer immediately after each question (train
generation, not verification); recognition masquerading as recall; only testing what
you already know to feel good; testing only after "finishing studying."

**Signs it's working:** You can predict your exam score; gaps feel small and specific;
recall gets faster and less effortful at the target level.
