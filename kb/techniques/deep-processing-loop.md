# The deep processing loop (pause, simplify, compare, connect, group, judge)

**What it's for:** The core moment-to-moment habit of a strong encoder: what to actually
*do* in your head, every time information comes in, so it lands in long-term memory as
organized, usable knowledge. Everything else in this knowledge base (mapping, PACER,
analogies) is scaffolding around this loop. Sung's framing: a genius does these steps
automatically; anyone can build the same habit deliberately.

**Why it works:** The brain encodes by placing new information into a relevance
structure, like shelving a book in a library. Information that fits nowhere is
aggressively discarded (holding irrelevant data is metabolically expensive), and
re-reading discarded information is "a shouting match with your brain" — that's rote
memorization, and every round of it is **learning debt** your future self repays. The
loop makes information relevant *the first time*, inside the 15–30 second working-memory
window where encoding actually happens.

**The loop — run it on every meaningful piece of information:**

1. **Pause.** Stop consuming. New input while you're still processing the last piece
   overloads working memory ("don't overeat information" — consumption and digestion
   must stay balanced). Stop the video, stop reading, and work with what's on the bench.
2. **Simplify.** Re-express it in plainer, more intuitive words — as if explaining to a
   10-year-old. Don't try to remember or understand it; trying to simplify *produces*
   remembering and understanding as side effects, and even a wrong simplification
   strengthens the eventual correct one.
3. **Compare.** Hold it against other new pieces and against prior knowledge: how is it
   similar, how is it different? Meaning *is* relationship — comparing is how the brain
   finds where something fits.
4. **Connect.** Make the links explicit: what does this influence, imply, depend on?
   Add it to the growing network (on paper — see mapping; notes are the friend who
   holds your plates).
5. **Group.** When connections accumulate into an overwhelming web, find the shared
   patterns and collapse them into named groups. Same connections, far more organized,
   far easier to hold. Re-grouping as new information arrives is not rework — it *is*
   the encoding.
6. **Judge.** Evaluate: which of these relationships matters most? What's the priority,
   the main point, the significance for the big picture? And challenge your structure —
   every grouping is a hypothesis, almost certainly wrong on the first pass; rigidity
   locks early mistakes into the foundation. Judging is what lifts processing from
   "connected" to genuinely evaluative, the top of Bloom's ladder.

Then let the next piece in and run it again. "Everything" is deliberate: not when it's
obvious or when you feel like it — every piece, as a habit. When a step fails ("I can't
see what this connects to"), that failure produces excellent, targeted questions — a
hallmark of strong learners.

**Training the habit:** It feels slow and effortful at first — that's desirable
difficulty, and it's the point ("get used to thinking hard"). Start by asking "how can
I apply this?" if full compare/connect feels like too much, then level up. Expect weeks
of deliberate practice before it starts running semi-automatically; from then on it
compounds for life.

**Common mistakes:** Consuming an hour of material before processing any of it;
skipping pause because slowing down "feels inefficient"; simplifying by shortening
instead of re-expressing; grouping once and defending the structure instead of
re-judging it; doing the loop only on hard parts (easy parts are the anchors).

**Signs it's working:** Confusion becomes specific ("these two seem similar but differ
here…") instead of global; retention after days without review jumps; questions get
sharper; the loop starts firing on its own.
