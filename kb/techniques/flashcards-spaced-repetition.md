# Flashcards & spaced repetition (powerful, narrow, easily abused)

**What it's for:** Rote memorization of exact, discrete facts — terminology, vocabulary,
drug doses, dates, formulas you must produce verbatim — and fast recall from a specific
trigger (e.g. a first responder recognizing life-threatening cues). Spaced repetition
works via the spacing effect: memory strengthens when retrieval happens after a delay.
The tools (flashcards, memory palaces, link methods) are very effective *for this
purpose*.

**When:** As a **last resort**, after encoding techniques have done what they can — for
the residue of genuinely arbitrary facts that just have to be memorized. Not for
conceptual understanding, relationships, or anything you'll need to use flexibly:
flashcards produce isolated, surface-level knowledge, and cue-dependent forgetting locks
memories to the card's exact wording.

**How (properly):**
1. Minimize what enters the deck: everything you can understand relationally instead of
   memorizing saves multiples of future time.
2. **Swap your cues regularly**, especially on cards you always get right. If you know
   the answer before finishing the question, you're matching words to words, not
   recalling knowledge. Rephrase the question; test the same fact from new angles.
3. Never count recognition as recall. Failing a card and thinking "oh yeah, that's
   right" after flipping is a failed rep — mark it wrong.
4. Worry more about recall quality than scheduling precision — the app's intervals
   matter far less than whether the rep is genuine effortful recall.

**The debt model:** Every card you make is a promise of future reps — roughly, a minute
of card-making buys several minutes of future reviewing. An hour of card creation per
day quietly schedules 20+ hours of the next week. Learners doing 5–6 hours of flashcards
daily got there by "solving" every problem with more cards. Rote volume scales
punishingly: one student who cut rote memorization from 90% of his material to 70%
halved his total study time (60 → 30 hours/week). Small reductions in rote load produce
huge time savings.

**The advanced strategy (Sung's Anki masterclass):** Structure long study sessions
(3–4+ hours) into three parts:

1. **Targeted review** (start of session): During the week, flag cards answered
   *incorrectly three times in a row* and (differently) cards answered *correctly three
   times in a row*. For the 3×-wrong cards: spend 5–10 minutes each going deeper —
   connect the fact to prior knowledge, build a network around it, create analogies —
   or decide you don't actually need it and delete it. For the 3×-right cards: **merge
   and upgrade** — combine related mastered cards into one higher-order question that
   tests the same facts *plus* their relationships. This shrinks the deck, builds
   networks, and prevents "memorizing the card instead of the knowledge."
2. **Consolidation** (main block): Encode the new material since the last long session
   with a nonlinear, relational method. Critically, **filter**: only facts that resist
   encoding go into flashcards. If you're generating more than ~100–150 cards/week, the
   encoding method is the problem, not the deck.
3. **Preview**: Prime upcoming material (see priming doc). If forced to choose between
   review and preview, choose preview — it prevents the forgetting that creates review
   debt in the first place.

Do the cards themselves as **microlearning**: 3–7 minute bursts in dead time (bus,
queue) are disproportionately effective and can absorb the whole week's reps without
scheduling anything.

**Why flashcards can't carry the system:** They test 1:1 (one prompt, one fact) — good
for direct recall, structurally bad for higher-order learning where concepts must be
combined; multi-concept cards get messy to grade. Memory excels when knowledge is
networked, and cards don't build networks — so an all-flashcard system manufactures a
mountain of rote work that better encoding would have made unnecessary. Active recall +
spaced repetition takes you from *bad to better* (strong evidence), but has rapidly
diminishing returns from *better to best* — for already-good students, more repetition
can even hurt, because repetition-heavy systems tax every fact 3–5 revisits forever.
Strong encoding flattens the forgetting curve from the start, cancelling most of those
revisits. The honest test: **if it works, you shouldn't have to do it five or six
times.** Needing many repetitions means the material is failing the brain's relevance
filter, and the repetition is compensation, not learning. And it's a common weapon, not
a secret one — flashcards are "wearing shoes to go outside": used by top achievers and
failing students alike.

**Spacing intervals (what actually matters):** Use expanding gaps (day → days → week →
weeks) — research mildly favors them, fixed intervals are never time-efficient, and
longer gaps both mimic real retrieval demands (the exam is a week away, not two days)
and reveal *which* things decay when. But don't fine-tune settings: **the flexibility
of your spacing is proportional to your encoding quality.** Well-encoded material
tolerates "roughly a week, give or take days" with only a few percent difference;
poorly encoded material forgets so fast it *forces* short fixed intervals — miss two
days and 600 cards fall due ("Anki hell"), which eats exactly the time needed to fix
the real problem, the encoding. Escaping that cycle: before making any card, spend a
moment comparing the idea against others and looking for an analogous relationship
that makes it obvious — if you find one, it doesn't need a card. Early on, rote may
still carry 90% of your load; as encoding skill grows, the deck shrinks toward the
genuinely arbitrary residue.

**Common mistakes:** Flashcards as the entire study system; carding conceptual material
that needed mapping or self-explanation; making cards as procrastination (creation isn't
learning); keeping stale cues; recognition-passing; dumping everything into the deck
instead of filtering at encoding time.

**Signs it's being used well:** The deck is small and genuinely arbitrary-facts-only;
reps feel like effortful recall; total flashcard time is a minor fraction of study time.
