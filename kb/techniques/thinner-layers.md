# Thinner layers (schema-first breakdown)

**What it's for:** Handling overwhelming material without destroying the connections
that make it learnable. The standard advice — "break it into smaller pieces" — is good
productivity advice but bad learning advice: splitting a topic into arbitrary chunks
(e.g. lecture by lecture) removes the very cross-connections the brain needs. Jigsaw
analogy: pulling a random handful of pieces out of the box doesn't make the puzzle
easier — the piece you're holding connects to pieces you just excluded.

**When:** A topic feels overwhelming because there are many concepts that obviously
relate somehow, but you can't see how. Multi-lecture course blocks covering one topic.

**How:** Break the material into **thinner layers**, not smaller pieces:
1. Scan through *everything* first — headings, titles, overview. List all the key
   concepts in a literal list.
2. Ask: *which of these seem related off the bat?* Connect the easiest, most obvious
   pairs first. That mini-network is your foundation layer.
3. Layer over it: with a small schema in place, pieces that made no sense before now
   have anchor points. Add them. Each addition makes the next easier — fewer pieces left
   in the box, and patterns become visible.
4. Repeat until everything is organized and connected. Going from unsorted pile to full
   structure in one pass overwhelms anyone; layers make the same journey tractable.

**Common mistakes:** Studying lecture 1 exhaustively before glancing at lectures 2–3
that cover the same topic (arbitrary division); treating the first layer as "too easy to
count" — the easy connections are the scaffold for the hard ones.

**Signs it's working:** Overwhelm drops after the first layer; late-arriving concepts
feel like filling gaps rather than starting over.
