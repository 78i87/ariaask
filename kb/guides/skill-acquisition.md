# Skill acquisition (RAIL, theory overload, professional learning)

Learning *skills* (coding, an instrument, public speaking, studying itself) differs from
learning knowledge chiefly through the **latent learning period** (Tolman): the delay
between doing the learning and getting feedback on it. Tying shoelaces gives instant
feedback; chess strategy or study technique may not show results for weeks — during
which hundreds of variables muddy the signal. The central question that makes skill
learning fast is *"am I moving in the right direction?"* — and the RAIL framework
(a practical repackaging of the four stages of competence) answers it.

## RAIL: match the action to the stage

Doing stage-3 actions while at stage 2 wastes time and guarantees frustration; aligned
actions make progression fast.

1. **R — Relevance** ("I don't know what to care about"). Signs: lost, can't even form
   questions; either nothing or everything seems worth working on. Progress = more
   variables becoming meaningful, not performing better. Actions: **exploration** (talk
   to people who are good at it, gather diverse opinions, learn the theory of what
   matters) and **challenging** your assumptions (what you think is key often isn't;
   the thing you're dismissing may be the main constraint).
2. **A — Awareness** (the plateau — where most people quit). You attempt the skill and
   fail repeatedly. Reframe: if mastering the skill requires making ~100 distinct
   mistakes, progress = making them as fast as possible. Actions: **experimentation**
   (attempt it; don't spend weeks pre-analyzing to avoid mistakes — that's paralysis)
   and **reflection** (what was the mistake, why?). For mistakes you can't even see,
   get feedback from someone who knows — everyone needs a coach at this stage.
3. **I — Iteration** (correct sometimes, inconsistent, slow, effortful). No new
   mistakes are appearing; the struggle is doing it right *every* time. Order matters:
   **accuracy → consistency → speed** — chasing speed before consistency just raises
   the error rate (a classic demotivation trap). Actions: **varied practice** (different
   contexts, difficulties, conditions — different subjects, tired days, time pressure)
   and **adjust** (fine-tune the technique so consistency survives non-ideal
   conditions).
4. **L — Lifelong** (it's a habit; doing it differently now takes effort). Actions:
   refine, and keep using it — neglected skills undergo **skill decay** and slip back
   down the stages.

## Theory overload: the ~100% failure mode

Every new thing you're consciously tracking consumes cognitive resources ("multiple
element interactivity"), on top of the already-high cost of performing an unfamiliar
skill. Take in a dozen techniques at once and you're juggling 20–30 elements —
guaranteed overload, zero skill formation. Sung's program data: the student who rushed
30% of the material in 5 weeks got *worse*; the one who took a year for 20% (waiting
for each skill to become habit before adding theory) jumped from 62% to 92%.

**The balance rule:** experiment with only **one or two new things at a time**, and
balance theory intake against habit formation — rule of thumb **1 hour of theory per
~5 hours of practice**, but the real signal is habit formation: when the skill gets
easier and faster *without* losing accuracy (speed arriving unchased), space has freed
up for new theory. Practice without theory is aimless; theory without practice is
overload. This is **experiential cycling**: experience → observe result → decide what
to change → experiment again. An arrow's landing is determined before release; firing
more arrows without adjusting the setup is not practice.

## Professional learning (no curriculum, no exams)

- **Start from zero.** University habits usually don't serve professional learning
  (often didn't serve then). Build from "what's my goal, what's my situation," not from
  old habits — the hard part is unlearning, and struggling students often outpace
  former straight-A students for exactly this reason.
- **Learn in sprints.** Set explicit learning objectives (what, how well, by when).
  Consume aggressively until you start losing track — often just hours — then **apply
  for an extended period** until the knowledge feels like your own. Only then start the
  next sprint. (Two weeks of reading can justify six months of applying.)
- **Lead, don't follow.** Learn *as if becoming the expert*: purpose shapes encoding,
  so aiming at expert-level questions organizes the knowledge the way an expert's is.
- **Tactically hit the books.** Treat studying as the fourth-choice solution to a
  knowledge gap (after: already know it → quick lookup → deep search). When you do
  study, target exactly the gap — don't passively complete a course hoping it covers it.
- **Delete the latent learning period.** Propose an application of what you just
  learned immediately and get it checked early and often; discovering a wrong mental
  model weeks later wastes everything built on it.
- **Slow is fast.** Consumption is never the bottleneck; processing is. Fast learners
  look slow — lots of pondering, little frantic page-turning.
- **Bring everything to the table.** Hold the thinking standard (map, judge, connect,
  question) in *all* work, not just study sessions — inconsistency prevents the habit
  from ever forming, and the habit is what makes it effortless.
- **Two overeating check questions** while learning anything: *Does this actually make
  sense to me?* and *Do I feel like I'm about to forget this?* Either answer bad →
  stop consuming and digest (see deep-processing-loop, PACER).

## Coaching cues

- Diagnose the RAIL stage before recommending anything — the same learner needs
  completely different actions at R (explore) vs A (attempt + reflect) vs I (varied
  practice).
- Learner consuming courses/books back-to-back → theory overload; cap new theory at
  1–2 elements, prescribe application.
- "I keep practicing but I'm not improving" → check the experiential cycle: are they
  reflecting and adjusting, or firing arrows?
- Learner speeding up and erroring → re-order: accuracy, consistency, *then* speed.
