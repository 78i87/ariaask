# The PERRIO system (systems over techniques; the relevance filter)

Asking "what's the best study technique?" reveals the asker is thinking in techniques,
not systems — and no one becomes an efficient learner that way. Sung's whole-system
frame is **PERRIO**: Priming, Encoding, Reference, Retrieval, Interleaving,
Overlearning. Each is a *slot* in the flow of learning; techniques fill slots.

## The relevance filter (why the system is ordered this way)

Information coming in is **filtered by relevance against what you already know**. If
the brain can't see how something connects, it discards it — no matter how many times
you re-insert it ("hunter-gatherers didn't need stone flashcard tablets at the fire
pit"; the brain evolved to keep what matters and aggressively dump the rest). Repetition
that "works" only after five or six passes isn't working — it's compensating for
failing the filter. Once information passes the filter and gets processed/organized, it
sits in memory, and each retrieval reprocesses and further organizes it. Weak process →
more repetition needed; strong process → less.

## The six slots

- **P — Priming**: anything done *before* a main learning event (class, lecture, long
  session) to pre-build relevance. Earliest in the flow, so failures here poison
  everything downstream (overload, leaky memory, weak retrieval). "You were thrown a
  ball and weren't ready to catch it."
- **E — Encoding**: the processing that makes sense of information and organizes it
  into memory — grouping, simplifying, analogies, connections, making it intuitive. The
  effortful part most people skip; a spectrum of efficiency, not a binary.
- **R — Reference**: a parking lot for fine details that would derail encoding —
  flashcards, a second brain (Obsidian/Notion), a glossary. Dump them, keep processing;
  come back later. (Note-taking as offload, not as learning.)
- **R — Retrieval**: testing use and application of knowledge; both a gap-check and a
  re-processing pass that strengthens memory.
- **I — Interleaving**: hitting the same topic from multiple angles so knowledge isn't
  locked to one cue or format (see the interleaving technique doc).
- **O — Overlearning**: repetition-heavy work beyond the required scope/standard for
  fast recall and fluency — lots of practice questions, lots of cards. **Optional**:
  only worth it for highly competitive assessments or very high excellence bars. The
  classic system failure is running overlearning strategies *first*, compensating for
  bad encoding with volume, which steals the time needed to fix priming/encoding/
  retrieval.

## Using it diagnostically

The coach's move: locate the weakest slot instead of tuning the strongest.
"Forget everything despite constant review" → encoding (and probably priming) failure,
not insufficient overlearning. "Understand in class, lost a week later" → no retrieval.
"Can answer the flashcard version only" → no interleaving. "Overwhelmed before starting"
→ no priming. "Encoding derailed by detail-chasing" → no reference habit.

## Coaching cues

- "What's the best technique?" → reframe to systems; walk PERRIO and find the gap.
- A learner whose entire system is one slot (all-flashcards, all-rereading) → name the
  empty slots, fill the highest-leverage one first.
- Overlearning is the last thing to add, not the first — and often unnecessary.
