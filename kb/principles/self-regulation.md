# Self-regulated learning (systems, illusions, logs)

## Studying is not learning

Studying is the physical activity (writing notes, reading, testing); learning is the
outcome in the brain — and learning has only occurred if you can **remember** the
material, **understand it deeply**, and **apply it** the way you need to. Ten hours of
studying with poor retention/understanding/application is ten hours of studying and zero
learning. Techniques sit on a spectrum of how much learning they produce per hour:
rereading and highlighting produce almost none; the job is to delete low-yield methods
and replace them with high-yield ones. Always evaluate "how much learning is each of my
methods producing?", not "am I studying enough?"

## No hacks; think systems, not techniques

Real study hacks don't exist — chronic learning problems are rooted in years of stacked
habits, and 30-second tricks can't fix them (chasing them mostly teaches learners to
falsely conclude they're not smart enough). There is also no perfect technique:
techniques are tools. You can't build a house with only a screwdriver; every technique
has a purpose and limitations. Two failure modes: abandoning a good tool because it has
limitations, and abusing one tool for everything (the classic: flashcards as the entire
study system). Upgrade the whole learning system — everyone already has one; it's just
unrefined.

## Don't copy successful students

Learning is personalized. Most naturally successful students succeed because their
**deep processing** (the brain's ability to connect information and extract meaning —
partly innate, very trainable) is strong enough that mediocre techniques still work for
them — a powerful engine forgives sloppy driving. Copying their techniques without their
processing gives you their habits and not their results. Train the processing itself.

## The illusion of learning

Anything that feels like progress but produces no measurable learning — the learning
equivalent of productive procrastination. Classic examples: spending 3 hours writing or
rewriting notes after a 1-hour lecture and being unable to answer a test question
afterwards; making hundreds of flashcards (feels great, but creating cards isn't
learning — see the flashcards doc on "flashcard debt"); recognition-based review
("oh yeah, that's right" after flipping the card — you just proved you *couldn't* recall
it). The exit is honest measurement: could I pass a test on this right now?

## Silly mistake syndrome

Assume every mistake will happen again. "That was just a silly mistake — I actually knew
it" is almost always false: understanding an answer sheet is not the same as being able
to generate the answer. Consistent "silly mistakes" nearly always mark genuine gaps or
misalignments in how the topic was studied. Treat each one as a real gap and dig until
you find why the missing piece was necessary.

## Study ahead to get ahead

Falling behind means current methods aren't keeping pace, so the backlog will only grow.
Plug the hole before bailing water: keep up with *new* material first (using scoping/
priming to make it cheap — see the priming doc), then chip away at the backlog.

## Keep a learning log

You can only change one or two habits at a time (the brain resists more), and improving
a learning system involves hundreds of factors — so it's a long series of experiments.
Log them: what am I testing, what do I expect, how did it go, what next. It compounds
improvement dramatically and is highly motivating in hindsight — you can see how far
your thinking has come. (This maps directly to this app's technique-usage tracking.)

## Coaching cues

- "Which technique is best?" → wrong question; what's the task, what's the stage, and
  what does the current system look like?
- Hours of note rewriting → illusion of learning; would you pass a test right now?
- "I keep making silly mistakes" → they're gaps; investigate each one.
- "I'm behind" → study ahead + scope; don't try to catch up linearly.
