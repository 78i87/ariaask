# Closed-book summarization ("sneaky plagiarism")

**What it's for:** Turning summarization — normally a low-utility technique — into a
genuine encoding + retrieval workout. Dunlosky's research rates summarization low
utility unless the learner is trained to do it well, and most people aren't: open-book
condensing of ten pages into one by deleting words, shortening sentences, and
bulletizing is *editorial* work, not analytical work. It builds no schema and involves
zero retrieval. (This is also why Cornell-style summarizing is overrated in practice.)

**When:** Consolidating after a reading/lecture; condensing notes before an exam;
any time a learner says "I'll summarize it" — upgrade the method.

**How:**
1. **Close the book.** Summarize from memory, not while looking at the source. This
   converts the exercise into free retrieval, exposing gaps and strengthening the memory.
2. **Restructure, don't shorten — "sneaky plagiarism."** Imagine the original author
   reading your summary: they shouldn't recognize it as derived from their text.
   Reorganize the material, rearrange concepts, explain connections between ideas in new
   ways, invent a framework or model that wasn't in the original. (Don't actually
   plagiarize — the point is the transformation.)
3. Check against the source afterwards and correct — errors found here are re-encoding
   opportunities.

**Common mistakes:** Summarizing with the source open (no retrieval); compressing
instead of reorganizing (no processing); treating a neat final page as the goal — the
learning happened in the restructuring, not the artifact.

**Signs it's working:** It feels harder than normal summarizing (desirable difficulty);
your summary's structure differs from the source's; gaps surface that you then fix.
